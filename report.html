Section 03

Regulatory Obligation Register

The following register maps each applicable regulation to the current compliance status derived from assessment responses. It is designed for use in board reporting, legal review, and audit defence preparation. Mandatory obligations carry financial penalties; deadlines are binding, not advisory.

Accountability Gap — Regulatory Blindspot
Under FCA PS7/21 and EU AI Act Art. 27, accountability for AI systems cannot be distributed across functions. The absence of a named individual responsible for AI governance creates a Regulatory Blindspot that prevents effective human oversight. In any supervisory inquiry, enforcement investigation, or litigation, regulators will identify a specific person to question. “Shared responsibility” is not a legally defensible position under either framework. MLA Group recommends the immediate appointment of a named AI Oversight Lead with documented mandate and veto authority over model deployment.

Section 06

Forward Risk Landscape

The following section addresses the forward trajectory of AI regulation over the next 12–36 months. Understanding the direction of regulatory travel is essential for boards making investment decisions about governance infrastructure.

The regulatory landscape governing AI is not static. The EU AI Act’s high-risk obligations, effective August 2026, represent the most significant compliance deadline facing organisations with material AI deployments. However, the enforcement trajectory extends well beyond this initial milestone. The European AI Office, established in February 2024, has commenced proactive monitoring of systemic risk models and has signalled an intent to pursue active enforcement from Q3 2026. The Commission’s proposed Digital Omnibus package in late 2025 could extend Annex III deadlines to December 2027 — but organisations should not assume this extension will materialise. Prudent compliance planning treats August 2026 as the binding deadline.
In the United Kingdom, the FCA published its AI Review conclusions confirming that AI governance is now a standing supervisory priority. The Bank of England’s Critical Third Party regime, operational since 2025, extends regulatory scrutiny to AI vendors supplying services to regulated firms. This means third-party AI tools embedded in business processes now carry indirect compliance obligations for the firms that use them — a material expansion of the regulatory perimeter that many organisations have not yet accounted for. Senior managers are now individually on the hook for AI-driven outcomes under SM&CR: the FCA has made clear that “reasonable steps” in relation to AI tools is a live supervisory expectation, not a future obligation.
ISO/IEC 42001, published in December 2023, is moving rapidly from a governance benchmark to a procurement requirement. Enterprise clients, public sector buyers, and regulated counterparties are increasingly requesting evidence of ISO 42001 compliance as part of supplier qualification and due diligence. For organisations at this governance maturity level, a certification-ready programme is a 12–18 month undertaking that must begin before it becomes an urgent commercial necessity. The organisations that begin now will hold a competitive governance advantage by 2027 that latecomers will find difficult and expensive to close.
